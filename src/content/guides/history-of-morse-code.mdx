## The Birth of the Telegraph

Before the 1830s, long-distance communication was limited by the speed of human travel. Letters took days or weeks to arrive via horse, ship, or train. The invention of the electrical telegraph fundamentally changed human history by allowing information to travel at the speed of electricity.

In 1832, an American portrait painter named **Samuel F.B. Morse** conceived the idea of a single-wire telegraph after hearing conversations about the newly discovered electromagnetism while sailing from Europe to America.

## Developing the Code

While Morse had the vision for the telegraph, he was not a trained scientist. He enlisted the help of Leonard Gale, a science professor, and **Alfred Vail**, a brilliant young machinist. 

It was Vail who significantly expanded and refined the system of dots and dashes we know today as American Morse Code. They designed the code based on the frequency of letters in the English language:
* The most common letter, **E**, was assigned the simplest symbol: a single dot (`.`).
* The second most common letter, **T**, became a single dash (`-`).
* Rarer letters like **Z** (`--..`) and **Q** (`--.-`) received longer, more complex sequences.

On May 24, 1844, Morse sent the first official telegraph message from the Supreme Court chamber in Washington, D.C., to the B&O Railroad Depot in Baltimore, Maryland. The message read: *"What hath God wrought?"* ([Library of Congress, Samuel Morse Papers](https://www.loc.gov/collections/samuel-morse-papers/about-this-collection/)).

## International Morse Code

As the telegraph spread globally, American Morse Code proved inadequate because it only supported the English alphabet. In 1851, a conference in Europe developed **International Morse Code** (also known as Continental Morse Code). 

This new standard removed the varied spacing of American Morse and standardized the lengths of dots and dashes, making it compatible with non-English European languages containing diacritic marks. This is the version of Morse Code still formally [standardized by the ITU](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-M.1677-1-200910-I/) and used worldwide today.

## Morse Code in the Modern Era

Though commercial telegraphs are obsolete and modern digital communication relies on [binary code](/guides/how-computers-read-binary), Morse Code remains relevant. It is heavily utilized by:
* **Aviation & Maritime:** Used as a fallback communication method and for navigational beacons like NDBs ([FAA Aeronautical Information Publication](https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aip_html/part2_enr_section_4.1.html)).
* **Amateur Radio (Ham):** Required for certain license classes in many countries and highly effective in poor signal conditions.
* **Accessibility:** Used as an assistive communication device for people with severe motor disabilities.
